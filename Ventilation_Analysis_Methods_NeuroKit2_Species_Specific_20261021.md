# Video based analysis of ventilatory frequency

Ventilatory frequency was quantified from video recordings of epaulette sharks and shovelnose rays using a custom Python workflow. The analysis was designed for stationary animals undergoing buccal or spiracular pumping and was not applied to recordings in which locomotion or an obscured respiratory field prevented identification of a repeatable cycle. Experimental folders and filenames encoded species, oxygen treatment, and drug treatment. The program required species labels identifying epaulette sharks or shovelnose rays and retained the original folder hierarchy and file name in the output table to support traceability.

Species specific respiratory regions of interest were selected interactively from representative recordings. For epaulette sharks, the region enclosed a clearly visible buccal or gill movement. For shovelnose rays, the region enclosed a visible spiracle or branchial movement. Each region was stored as a proportion of image width and height, allowing it to be applied to recordings with the same within species camera geometry. Separate regions were selected for the two species because the visible respiratory landmarks differ. Files with an unrecognised species label were not assigned a ventilation estimate.

Each recording was decoded frame by frame with OpenCV. Frames were converted to greyscale, and a respiratory motion trace was calculated as the mean absolute difference in pixel intensity between successive frames within the relevant region of interest. The trace was smoothed using a centred moving average of approximately 70 ms, linearly detrended, and scaled by its standard deviation. A Hann window was then applied and the dominant component of the one sided fast Fourier transform within a prespecified physiologically plausible range of 20 to 180 cycles min<sup>−1</sup> was used to obtain a spectral estimate of ventilatory frequency.

Putative respiratory contractions were identified from the standardised motion trace with the NeuroKit2 general signal peak detection function, `signal_findpeaks` (Makowski et al., 2021). This approach was selected because the signal is an image derived motion trace rather than a conventional respiratory belt recording. Candidate peaks were filtered using a relative peak height threshold of 0.0, and a minimum separation of 70% of the period indicated by the dominant spectral component was imposed to avoid counting multiple extrema within one cycle. For recordings with at least two accepted peaks, the interval estimate was calculated as 60 divided by the mean interpeak interval. The primary automated estimate was the mean of the Fourier and interpeak interval estimates. When fewer than two acceptable peaks were available, the Fourier estimate was retained and the result was flagged accordingly.

The program recorded frame rate, image dimensions, video duration, spectral estimate, NeuroKit2 peak estimate, number of accepted peaks, interpeak coefficient of variation, final frequency estimate, processing method, and processing status in a comma separated file. Diagnostic plots showing the raw and smoothed motion trace, standardised detrended trace, and detected events were retained for visual quality control. Recordings shorter than 5 s or 30 frames, recordings with a constant motion trace, incomplete video metadata, a zero area region, an unrecognised species label, or an unresolved spectral band were retained in the results table with a failure status rather than assigned a physiological value.

Before inferential analysis, automated estimates should be compared with blinded manual counts from a stratified set of videos spanning each species, oxygen treatment, drug treatment, body orientation, camera geometry, and expected rate range. Agreement should be reported using a prespecified metric such as mean bias and limits of agreement or an intraclass correlation coefficient. This validation is particularly important because epaulette sharks and shovelnose rays differ in visible respiratory anatomy and in their responses to hypoxia. The present pipeline measures the periodicity of a selected visible respiratory movement and should not be interpreted as a direct measurement of minute ventilation or oxygen uptake.
