# Ventilation video analysis

Ventilation rate was estimated from video recordings using a custom local Python application built with OpenCV and NumPy. Video files were processed from a locally synchronised Dropbox directory. The application recursively identified recordings in MP4, MOV, AVI, MKV, M4V, and WMV formats and created one record per file. Experimental labels were inferred from folder names and file names using predefined keywords for species (shark or ray), oxygen condition (hypoxia or normoxia), and treatment (PBS or Poly). These automatically assigned labels were checked against the experimental record before statistical analysis.

A rectangular region of interest encompassing the visible buccal or gill area was selected interactively from a representative recording. The region was stored as a proportion of frame width and height and applied consistently to videos acquired with comparable orientation and framing. Each video was decoded frame by frame. Frames were converted to greyscale and the ventilation motion signal was calculated as the mean absolute pixel intensity difference between consecutive frames within the region of interest. The first frame was assigned a motion value of zero.

For each recording, the motion trace was smoothed using a centred moving average with a window of approximately 70 ms, detrended by subtraction of a least squares linear fit, and scaled by its standard deviation. A Hann window was applied before calculating the one sided fast Fourier transform. The dominant frequency within a prespecified physiologically plausible range of 20 to 180 breaths min<sup>−1</sup> was converted to breaths min<sup>−1</sup> to provide a spectral estimate of ventilation rate. The detrended trace was also subjected to local maximum detection. Candidate peaks were required to exceed a normalised prominence threshold of 0.5 and were separated by at least 70% of the period predicted from the dominant spectral frequency. When two or more peaks were detected, a second estimate was calculated as 60 divided by the mean interval between adjacent peaks. The reported ventilation rate was the mean of the spectral and peak interval estimates. Where fewer than two peaks were detected, the spectral estimate alone was reported.

Videos shorter than 5 s or 30 frames, recordings with incomplete metadata, a zero area region of interest, constant motion signal, or an unresolvable sampling rate were flagged by the software and retained in the output file with an error status rather than assigned a ventilation value. The comma separated results file retained folder hierarchy, file name, inferred experimental labels, frame rate, frame dimensions, recording duration, spectral estimate, peak interval estimate, number of detected peaks, interpeak coefficient of variation, final estimate, and processing status. For quality control, diagnostic plots showing raw and smoothed motion traces, the standardised detrended trace, and detected peaks were generated for each recording and reviewed before downstream analysis.

Automated ventilation estimates should be validated against blinded manual counts from a stratified subset of recordings spanning both species, oxygen conditions, treatments, recording geometries, and expected rate ranges. The final manuscript should report the number of validation recordings and the agreement statistic selected before analysis, for example mean bias with limits of agreement or intraclass correlation coefficient. Recordings with obscured respiratory structures, substantial animal movement, or poor agreement with manual counts should be excluded using criteria defined before group comparisons.
